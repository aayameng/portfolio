Stirling’s approximation

The goal

We want a simple estimate of $n!$ for large $n$. The target is Stirling’s formula:

$$n!\;\approx\;\sqrt{2\pi n}\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^{n}\tag{1}$$

Here “$\approx$” means the ratio of the two sides tends to $1$ as $n\to\infty$. The plan (Laplace’s method): write $n!$ as an integral, find the sharp peak of the integrand, approximate it by a Gaussian, and integrate.

For real $z>0$ the Gamma function is

$$\Gamma(z)=\int_0^\infty t^{z-1}e^{-t}\,dt.\tag{2}$$

Lemma 1. $\Gamma(z+1)=z\,\Gamma(z)$, and therefore $\Gamma(n+1)=n!$ for every integer $n\ge0$.

Proof. Integrate (2) by parts with $u=t^{z}$ and $dv=e^{-t}dt$:

$$\begin{aligned}\Gamma(z+1)&=\Big[-t^{z}e^{-t}\Big]_0^\infty+z\int_0^\infty t^{z-1}e^{-t}\,dt\\&=z\,\Gamma(z).\end{aligned}\tag{3}$$

The boundary term vanishes: $t^ze^{-t}\to0$ as $t\to\infty$ (the exponential wins) and as $t\to0^+$ (since $z>0$). Since $\Gamma(1)=\int_0^\infty e^{-t}dt=1$, repeated use of (3) gives

$$\begin{aligned}\Gamma(n+1)&=n\,\Gamma(n)=n(n-1)\,\Gamma(n-1)\\&=\cdots=n!\,\Gamma(1)=n!\end{aligned}\tag{4}$$

□

Move it into the exponent

Setting $z=n+1$ in (2) and using Lemma 1,

$$n!=\int_0^\infty x^{n}e^{-x}\,dx.\tag{5}$$

Write $x^n=e^{n\ln x}$, so the whole integrand becomes a single exponential:

$$\begin{aligned}n!&=\int_0^\infty e^{\,n\ln x-x}\,dx=\int_0^\infty e^{f(x)}\,dx,\\f(x)&=n\ln x-x.\end{aligned}\tag{6}$$

An exponential is dominated by the region where its exponent is largest. This is the idea behind Laplace’s method, so we study the maximum of $f$.

Find the peak

Lemma 2. For $n>0$, $f(x)=n\ln x-x$ has a unique maximum on $(0,\infty)$ at $x=n$, with $f(n)=n\ln n-n$ and $f''(n)=-1/n$.

Proof. Differentiate:

$$f'(x)=\frac{n}{x}-1.\tag{7}$$

This vanishes only at $x=n$. For $0<x<n$ we have $f'(x)>0$, and for $x>n$ we have $f'(x)<0$, so $f$ increases up to $x=n$ and decreases afterwards. The second derivative is

$$f''(x)=-\frac{n}{x^{2}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad f''(n)=-\frac1n<0,\tag{8}$$

which confirms a maximum. Finally $f(n)=n\ln n-n$ by direct substitution.

□

Expand around the peak

By Taylor’s theorem about $x=n$, for some $\xi$ between $x$ and $n$,

$$\begin{aligned}f(x)&=f(n)+f'(n)(x-n)\\&\quad+\frac{f''(n)}{2}(x-n)^2+\frac{f'''(\xi)}{6}(x-n)^3.\end{aligned}\tag{9}$$

From Lemma 2 the first three coefficients are

$$\begin{gathered}f(n)=n\ln n-n,\\f'(n)=0,\qquad f''(n)=-\frac1n.\end{gathered}\tag{10}$$

The third derivative is $f'''(x)=2n/x^{3}$, so the cubic coefficient is $f'''(n)/6=1/(3n^2)$. Dropping the cubic term and inserting (10) into (9):

$$f(x)\approx(n\ln n-n)-\frac{(x-n)^2}{2n}.\tag{11}$$

Why drop the cubic term? The integrand is negligible unless $|x-n|$ is a few multiples of $\sqrt n$. On that scale the quadratic term $(x-n)^2/2n$ is of order $1$, while the cubic term $(x-n)^3/3n^2$ is of order $n^{-1/2}\to0$.

The Gaussian approximation

Substitute (11) into (6):

$$n!\approx\int_0^\infty\exp\!\left[n\ln n-n-\frac{(x-n)^2}{2n}\right]dx.\tag{12}$$

The factor $e^{n\ln n-n}=n^ne^{-n}$ does not depend on $x$, so it comes out front:

$$n!\approx n^{n}e^{-n}\int_0^\infty e^{-(x-n)^2/(2n)}\,dx.\tag{13}$$

The remaining integrand is a bell curve with mean $n$ and variance $\sigma^2=n$, that is, width $\sqrt n$.

Solve the Gaussian integral

Let $t=x-n$, so $dt=dx$ and the lower limit $x=0$ becomes $t=-n$:

$$\int_0^\infty e^{-(x-n)^2/(2n)}dx=\int_{-n}^{\infty}e^{-t^2/(2n)}\,dt.\tag{14}$$

Lemma 3. The tail of the Gaussian beyond $t=-n$ satisfies

$$0<\int_{-\infty}^{-n}e^{-t^2/(2n)}dt\le2e^{-n/2}.$$

Proof. For $t\le-n$ we have $t^2=|t|\,|t|\ge n|t|$, so $e^{-t^2/2n}\le e^{-|t|/2}$ and

$$\begin{aligned}\int_{-\infty}^{-n}e^{-t^2/(2n)}dt&\le\int_{n}^{\infty}e^{-u/2}\,du\\&=2e^{-n/2}.\end{aligned}\tag{15}$$

□

This tail is exponentially small, so the lower limit can be pushed to $-\infty$:

$$\begin{aligned}\int_{-n}^{\infty}e^{-t^2/(2n)}dt&=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-t^2/(2n)}dt\\&\quad-O\!\left(e^{-n/2}\right).\end{aligned}\tag{16}$$

For the full integral, let $a>0$ and $J=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-at^2}dt$. Squaring and passing to polar coordinates:

$$\begin{aligned}J^2&=\int\!\!\int_{\mathbb R^2}e^{-a(s^2+t^2)}\,ds\,dt\\&=\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^\infty e^{-ar^2}\,r\,dr\,d\theta\\&=2\pi\left[-\frac{e^{-ar^2}}{2a}\right]_0^\infty=\frac{\pi}{a}.\end{aligned}\tag{17}$$

So $J=\sqrt{\pi/a}$, and with $a=1/(2n)$:

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-t^2/(2n)}dt=\sqrt{2\pi n}.\tag{18}$$

Stirling’s formula

Theorem. As $n\to\infty$, $\;n!\sim\sqrt{2\pi n}\,(n/e)^{n}$.

Derivation. Combine (13), (16) and (18):

$$\boxed{\begin{aligned}n!&\approx n^{n}e^{-n}\sqrt{2\pi n}\\&=\sqrt{2\pi n}\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^{n}\end{aligned}}\tag{19}$$

The one step not made exact above is the control of the cubic remainder in (9). A fully rigorous proof bounds it uniformly.

Remark. (19) is the first term of the asymptotic series

$$\begin{aligned}n!&=\sqrt{2\pi n}\left(\frac ne\right)^{n}\bigg(1+\frac1{12n}\\&\qquad+\frac1{288n^2}-\frac{139}{51840\,n^3}+\cdots\bigg),\end{aligned}\tag{20}$$

so the relative error of (19) is about $1/(12n)$, already below $1\%$ at $n=10$.
