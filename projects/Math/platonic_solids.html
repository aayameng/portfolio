The Geometric Proof

To prove that exactly and only five Platonic solids can exist, we analyze the geometric conditions required to form a closed, three-dimensional corner (a vertex).

Step 1: The Vertex Condition

A Platonic solid is constructed from congruent regular polygons. Let $p$ be the number of edges of each face (the polygon type), and $q$ be the number of faces meeting at each vertex. For a valid 3D vertex to form and fold inward to create a solid, the sum of the interior angles around a single vertex must be strictly less than a full circle ($360^\circ$ or $2\pi$ radians).

Step 2: Interior Angles

To find the interior angle of any regular $p$-sided polygon, we can divide it into $(p-2)$ triangles by drawing diagonals from a single vertex. Since every triangle contains $180^\circ$, the total sum of the polygon's interior angles is $(p-2) \times 180^\circ$.

Dividing this total evenly among all $p$ vertices gives the interior angle $\theta$:

$$\theta = \frac{(p-2) \times 180^\circ}{p}$$

Since exactly $q$ faces meet at each vertex in a Platonic Solid, the sum of their angles is $q \times \theta$. For a corner to fold out of flat 2D space into 3D, this sum must be strictly less than a full circle ($360^\circ$):

$$q \times \frac{(p-2) \times 180^\circ}{p} < 360^\circ$$

Step 3: Algebraic Reduction

Dividing both sides of our inequality by $180^\circ$ yields:

$$\frac{q(p-2)}{p} < 2$$

Multiplying by $p$ (since polygons must have positive edge counts):

$$qp - 2q < 2p$$

Rearranging all variables to the left side:

$$qp - 2q - 2p < 0$$

Adding $4$ to both sides allows us to factor the polynomial elegantly:

$$qp - 2q - 2p + 4 < 4$$
$$(p-2)(q-2) < 4$$

Step 4: Finding Integer Solutions

By geometric definition, a valid polygon must have at least 3 sides ($p \ge 3$), and a 3D corner requires at least 3 intersecting faces ($q \ge 3$). Therefore, $(p-2) \ge 1$ and $(q-2) \ge 1$. We evaluate all positive integer pairs where their product is strictly less than 4.

  • If $p = 3$ (Triangles):
    $$ \begin{aligned} (3-2)(q-2) &< 4 \\ \implies q - 2 &< 4 \\ \implies q &< 6 \end{aligned} $$
    The only valid integers $\ge 3$ are $q=3$ (Tetrahedron), $q=4$ (Octahedron), and $q=5$ (Icosahedron).
  • If $p = 4$ (Squares):
    $$ \begin{aligned} (4-2)(q-2) &< 4 \\ \implies 2(q-2) &< 4 \\ \implies q - 2 &< 2 \\ \implies q &< 4 \end{aligned} $$
    The only valid integer $\ge 3$ is $q=3$ (Cube).
  • If $p = 5$ (Pentagons):
    $$ \begin{aligned} (5-2)(q-2) &< 4 \\ \implies 3(q-2) &< 4 \\ \implies q - 2 &< 1.33 \\ \implies q &< 3.33 \end{aligned} $$
    The only valid integer $\ge 3$ is $q=3$ (Dodecahedron).
  • If $p \ge 6$ (Hexagons or greater): We know $(p-2) \ge 4$. Since $q \ge 3$, their product $(p-2)(q-2) \ge 4 \times 1 = 4$. This fundamentally violates the condition that the product must be strictly $< 4$. Thus, zero solutions exist for hexagons or higher-order polygons.

Conclusion: These absolute mathematical constraints dictate that exactly and only five regular, convex polyhedra can exist in our three-dimensional universe. Q.E.D.
