\(\int\sqrt{\tan x}\,dx\)

Geometry + complex poles, step by step, with every construction explained

\[\tan x=u^2\;\Rightarrow\;\sqrt{\tan x}=u,\qquad x=\arctan u^2\]
\[\sec^2x\,dx=2u\,du,\qquad\sec^2x=1+\tan^2x=1+u^4\]
\[dx=\frac{2u\,du}{1+u^4}\]
\[\sqrt{\tan x}\,dx=u\cdot\frac{2u\,du}{1+u^4}=\frac{2u^2}{1+u^4}\,du\]
\[\int_0^x\!\sqrt{\tan t}\,dt=\int_0^u\frac{2s^2}{1+s^4}\,ds,\qquad x:0\to\tfrac{\pi}{2}\;\Leftrightarrow\;u:0\to\infty\]
\[u^2=\tau^2-(\tau-1)^2=2\tau-1=\tan x\]
\[a\cos\theta=b\sin\theta=u,\qquad a\sin\theta=1,\qquad b\cos\theta=\tan x\]
\[u^2=(a\cos\theta)(b\sin\theta)=ab\sin\theta\cos\theta=(a\sin\theta)(b\cos\theta)=1\cdot\tan x\]

Draw a semicircle on a diameter of length \(1+\tan x\), so its radius is \(\tau=\tfrac{1+\tan x}{2}\). Where the segments \(1\) and \(\tan x\) meet, Pythagoras gives the height \(u=\sqrt{1\cdot\tan x}\). Joining the top to both ends gives a right triangle (legs \(a,b\), angle \(\theta\) at the top) whose altitude is \(u\); multiplying its trigonometry gives the same result. Drawn upright, \(u\) is still a real length.

Geometry on the canvas
  • the unit segment and the segment \(\tan x=u^2\) (drawn as 2.25) are laid end to end, so the diameter is \(1+\tan x\).
  • The cream semicircle has its centre at the midpoint (radius \(\tau\), dashed). By Thales’ theorem every point of it sees the diameter at \(90^\circ\).
  • the perpendicular at the junction meets the arc at height \(u\). In the right triangle the altitude to the hypotenuse is the geometric mean: \(u^2=1\cdot\tan x\). Pythagoras in the centre triangle gives the same.
  • Small squares mark the right angles at the foot and apex; \(\theta\) is the angle between leg \(a\) and the altitude.
\[U=u\,e^{i\pi/4}\;\Rightarrow\;U^2=i\,u^2,\qquad U^4=u^4e^{i\pi}=-u^4\]
\[dU=e^{i\pi/4}\,du\]
\[1+u^4=1-U^4=(1-U^2)(1+U^2)=-(U-1)(U+1)(U-i)(U+i)\]
\[u^4=-1\;\Rightarrow\;u=e^{i\pi/4+ik\pi/2}\;\Rightarrow\;U=e^{i\pi/2+ik\pi/2}\in\{i,-1,-i,1\}\]

The four poles of the integrand sit at \(u=e^{\pm i\pi/4},\,e^{\pm 3i\pi/4}\). Turning the plane by \(45^\circ\) moves them to \(\pm1,\pm i\), and \(u\), a real number that starts on the real axis, now runs along the \(45^\circ\) line through \(U\).

Geometry on the canvas
  • the four poles of \(1/(1+u^4)\) are the corners of a square inscribed in the unit circle, at \(45^\circ,135^\circ,225^\circ,315^\circ\).
  • A rotation about the origin is rigid: lengths and angles do not change, so the square simply turns until its corners sit on the axes at \(1,i,-1,-i\).
  • The dashed real axis becomes the gold ray at \(45^\circ\). The white point \(U=u\,e^{i\pi/4}\) slides along it and stays exactly \(u\) from the origin.
\[U-P=r\,e^{i\theta}\;\Rightarrow\;U=P+r\,e^{i\theta}\]
\[dU=e^{i\theta}\,dr+i\,r\,e^{i\theta}\,d\theta=e^{i\theta}\,(dr+i\,r\,d\theta)\]
\[\frac{dU}{U-P}=\frac{e^{i\theta}(dr+i\,r\,d\theta)}{r\,e^{i\theta}}\]
\[\boxed{\frac{dU}{U-P}=\frac{dr}{r}+i\,d\theta}\]
\[\operatorname{Re}\frac{dU}{U-P}=\frac{dr}{r}\ \text{(stretch)},\qquad\operatorname{Im}\frac{dU}{U-P}=d\theta\ \text{(turn)}\]
\[\text{scale the small triangle by }\tfrac1r:\qquad\text{hypotenuse }\frac{dU}{r},\quad ST=\frac{dr}{r},\quad TU'=i\,d\theta\]

Split the small step \(dU\) into a part along the radius (\(dr\), gold) and a part along the circle around \(P\) (\(r\,d\theta\), blue). Shrinking this triangle by \(1/r\) puts it on the unit circle around \(P\), at the tip of the unit vector \(\hat e_r\): its legs are \(ST=dr/r\) and \(TU'=i\,d\theta\). Equivalently, \(d\ln(U-P)=d\ln r+i\,d\theta\).

Geometry on the canvas
  • \(P\) is one pole. The blue dashed arc is the circle of radius \(r\) about \(P\) through \(U\): moving along it changes only \(\theta\); moving along the ray \(PU\) changes only \(r\).
  • The white step \(dU\) splits into a radial leg \(dr\) and a tangential leg \(r\,d\theta\): a right triangle, because a circle’s tangent is perpendicular to its radius.
  • Shrinking by \(1/r\) moves a copy to the unit circle about \(P\), with vertices \(S,T,U'\). Shrinking keeps every angle, and the quarter-turn of the tangent leg supplies the factor \(i\).
  • So real part = relative stretch along the ray, imaginary part = turn around \(P\).
\[P_1=1,\;P_2=-1,\;P_3=i,\;P_4=-i,\qquad U-P_k=r_k\,e^{i\theta_k},\quad\hat e_k=e^{i\theta_k}\]
\[dU=\hat e_k\,(dr_k+i\,r_k\,d\theta_k)\qquad(k=1,2,3,4)\]
\[dU=\frac14\sum_{k=1}^{4}\hat e_k\,(dr_k+i\,r_k\,d\theta_k)\]
\[a_k=\frac{dU}{U-P_k}=\frac{dr_k}{r_k}+i\,d\theta_k\]

Each pole gets its own unit circle, and \(\hat e_k\) points from the pole to \(U\). Any single pole is enough to push \(U\) forward, so the four little vectors \(a_k\) are the relative changes of the same \(dU\) seen from each pole. We can now add them in whatever combination is useful.

Geometry on the canvas
  • Each pole has its own unit circle and a dashed ray to \(U\): \(+1\), \(-1\), \(+i\), \(-i\).
  • The step \(dU\) is the same arrow everywhere, but each pole splits it differently into radial and tangential parts, so each pole gets its own shrunk triangle at \(S_k=P_k+\hat e_k\).
  • The shrunk hypotenuse is \(a_k=dU/(U-P_k)\). A near pole (\(\pm1\) or \(+i\) region) gives a long \(a_k\); a far pole gives a short one. \(U\) is drawn at \(u=2\) here so the four triangles stay apart.
\[\frac{dU}{U-1}-\frac{dU}{U+1}=\frac{2\,dU}{U^2-1},\qquad\frac{dU}{U-i}-\frac{dU}{U+i}=\frac{2i\,dU}{U^2+1}\]
\[A=\operatorname{Re}\Big(\frac{dU}{U-1}-\frac{dU}{U+1}\Big)=\frac{dr_1}{r_1}-\frac{dr_2}{r_2}\]
\[B=\operatorname{Im}\Big(\frac{dU}{U-i}-\frac{dU}{U+i}\Big)=d\theta_3+d\theta_4=d\varphi\]
\[\theta_3=\arg(U-i)+\tfrac{\pi}{2},\qquad\theta_4=\tfrac{\pi}{2}-\arg(U+i)\qquad\text{interior angles at }i\text{ and }-i\]
\[\frac{2\,dU}{U^2-1}=\frac{2e^{i\pi/4}\,du}{iu^2-1}=\frac{2e^{i\pi/4}(-1-iu^2)}{1+u^4}\,du=\frac{\sqrt2\,[(u^2-1)-i(u^2+1)]}{1+u^4}\,du\]
\[\frac{2i\,dU}{U^2+1}=\frac{2i\,e^{i\pi/4}\,du}{1+iu^2}=\frac{\sqrt2\,[(u^2-1)+i(u^2+1)]}{1+u^4}\,du\]
\[A=\frac{\sqrt2\,(u^2-1)}{1+u^4}\,du,\qquad B=\frac{\sqrt2\,(u^2+1)}{1+u^4}\,du\]
\[A+B=\frac{2\sqrt2\,u^2}{1+u^4}\,du\;\Rightarrow\;\boxed{\int\!\sqrt{\tan x}\,dx=\frac1{\sqrt2}\Big(\int A+\int B\Big)}\]

The two complex differentials are conjugates: they share the real part \(A\) and have opposite imaginary parts \(\mp B\). This choice of \(a_k\) came from trying a few combinations: it needs the least scaffolding to get back to the original integral, though other combinations work too.

Geometry on the canvas
  • Four rays join \(U\) to the poles. Their lengths are \(r_1\), \(r_2\) (for \(\pm1\)); their directions give the angles \(\theta_3\), \(\theta_4\) (for \(\pm i\)).
  • \(A\) uses the pair on the real axis: it measures how much the ratio \(r_1/r_2\) changes. \(B\) uses the pair on the imaginary axis: it measures how much the angle \(\varphi=\theta_3+\theta_4\) turns.
  • Taking a difference for \(\pm1\) and a sum of angles for \(\pm i\) cancels the unwanted real or imaginary parts by symmetry.
\[A+B=\operatorname{Re}\Big(\frac{dU}{U-1}-\frac{dU}{U+1}\Big)+\operatorname{Im}\Big(\frac{dU}{U-i}-\frac{dU}{U+i}\Big)=\Big(\frac{dr_1}{r_1}-\frac{dr_2}{r_2}\Big)+d\varphi\]
\[PQ=A\cos45^\circ+B\sin45^\circ=\frac{A+B}{\sqrt2}\]
\[PQ=\frac{\sqrt2\,(u^2-1)+\sqrt2\,(u^2+1)}{\sqrt2\,(1+u^4)}\,du=\frac{2u^2}{1+u^4}\,du=\sqrt{\tan x}\,dx\]
\[\boxed{\int\!\sqrt{\tan x}\,dx=\int_0^u\frac{2u^2\,du}{1+u^4}=\frac{\int A+\int B}{\sqrt2}}\]

Put \(A\) along the real axis and \(B\) along the imaginary axis, then project \(A+iB\) onto the \(45^\circ\) line where \(u\) lives. The projection of a sum is the sum of the projections, so the length \(PQ\) is exactly the original differential \(\sqrt{\tan x}\,dx\). We already know the answer's format: \(\int A\) is a log and \(\int B\) is an angle. The next two steps build each one from scratch.

Geometry on the canvas
  • \(A\) is drawn along the real axis (gold) and \(B\) upward from its tip (pink); the white arrow is \(A+iB\).
  • The dashed \(U\)-axis is the \(45^\circ\) line. Dotted perpendiculars drop from the tip of \(A\) and from the tip of \(A+iB\) onto it.
  • The projection of \(A\) is \(A\cos45^\circ\); the projection of \(iB\) is \(B\sin45^\circ\). Laid end to end they form \(PQ=(A+B)/\sqrt2\), because projection is additive.
\[h=u\cos45^\circ=u\sin45^\circ=\frac{u}{\sqrt2},\qquad U-i=h+i(h-1),\qquad U+i=h+i(h+1)\]
\[\tan\theta_3=\frac{h}{1-h},\qquad\tan\theta_4=\frac{h}{1+h}\]
\[\tan(\theta_3+\theta_4)=\frac{\tan\theta_3+\tan\theta_4}{1-\tan\theta_3\tan\theta_4}=\frac{2h/(1-h^2)}{(1-2h^2)/(1-h^2)}=\frac{2h}{1-2h^2}=\frac{\sqrt2\,u}{1-u^2}\]
\[r_4^2=h^2+(1+h)^2,\qquad\sin\theta_4=\frac{h}{r_4},\qquad\cos\theta_4=\frac{1+h}{r_4}\]
\[CD=2\sin\theta_4=\frac{\sqrt2\,u}{r_4},\qquad UC=r_4-2\cos\theta_4=\frac{u^2-1}{r_4}\]
\[\tan(\pi-\varphi)=\frac{CD}{UC}=\frac{\sqrt2\,u}{u^2-1}\;\Rightarrow\;\tan\varphi=\frac{\sqrt2\,u}{1-u^2}\]
\[\boxed{\int B=\varphi=\arctan\frac{\sqrt2\,u}{1-u^2}}\quad(0\le u<1)\]
\[\varphi\big|_{u=1}=\tfrac{\pi}{2},\qquad\varphi=\pi+\arctan\frac{\sqrt2\,u}{1-u^2}\quad(u>1)\]

Triangle \((i,\,U,\,-i)\) has angles \(\theta_3,\theta_4\) at the poles and \(\pi-\varphi\) at \(U\). Check: \(u=1\) puts \(U\) on the unit circle, the chord from \(i\) to \(-i\) is a diameter, so the angle at \(U\) is \(\pi/2\) and \(\varphi=\pi/2\). Past \(u=1\) the arctangent is continued so \(\varphi\) keeps rising to \(\pi\). Second proof: drop the perpendicular from the pole \(D=i\) to the line through \(-i\) and \(U\), meeting it at \(C\). In the right triangle at \(C\), the diameter \(2\) gives \(CD=2\sin\theta_4\) and \(UC=r_4-2\cos\theta_4\); their ratio is \(\tan(\pi-\varphi)\).

Geometry on the canvas
  • The triangle \((i,\,U,\,-i)\) has angle \(\theta_3\) at \(i\), \(\theta_4\) at \(-i\) and \(\pi-\varphi\) at \(U\). Angles of a triangle add to \(\pi\), so \(\varphi=\theta_3+\theta_4\).
  • The dashed horizontal from \(U\) to the axis has length \(h=u/\sqrt2\). It cuts two right triangles with vertical legs \(1-h\) and \(1+h\), which give \(\tan\theta_3\) and \(\tan\theta_4\).
  • Second proof: the perpendicular from \(i\) meets line \((-i,U)\) at \(C\) (right-angle mark). \(CD=2\sin\theta_4\), \(UC=r_4-2\cos\theta_4\).
  • Here \(u=1.35\), so \(U\) lies just outside the unit circle and the angle at \(U\) is below \(90^\circ\). At \(u=1\) it is exactly \(90^\circ\) (Thales), so \(\varphi=\pi/2\).
\[r\to r\Big(1+\frac{dr}{r}\Big)\;\Rightarrow\;\prod\Big(1+\frac{dr}{r}\Big)=e^{\int dr/r}\]
\[r_2=1\cdot\prod\Big(1+\frac{dr_1}{r_1}\Big)\cdot\prod\Big(1+\frac{dr_2}{r_2}-\frac{dr_1}{r_1}\Big)=e^{\int dr_1/r_1}\,e^{\int(dr_2/r_2-dr_1/r_1)}\]
\[\frac{r_2}{r_1}=e^{\int(dr_2/r_2-dr_1/r_1)}=e^{-\int A}\]
\[\int A=\ln r_1-\ln r_2=\ln\frac{r_1}{r_2}\quad\Big(\text{zero at }u=0,\ \text{where }r_1=r_2=1\Big)\]
\[r_1^2=\Big(\tfrac{u}{\sqrt2}-1\Big)^2+\tfrac{u^2}{2}=u^2-\sqrt2\,u+1\]
\[r_2^2=\Big(\tfrac{u}{\sqrt2}+1\Big)^2+\tfrac{u^2}{2}=u^2+\sqrt2\,u+1\]
\[\boxed{\int A=\frac12\ln\frac{u^2-\sqrt2\,u+1}{u^2+\sqrt2\,u+1}}\]

Seen from \(r_1\) as the unit, \(r_2\) is built by the factor \(1+\big(\tfrac{dr_2}{r_2}-\tfrac{dr_1}{r_1}\big)\). The unit circles around \(\pm1\) hold the scaled triangles \(dr_1/r_1\) and \(dr_2/r_2\), and their difference is \(A\). Check by differentiating: the common denominator is \((u^2+1)^2-2u^2=1+u^4\) and the numerator collapses to \(2\sqrt2\,(u^2-1)\), so the derivative is \(\tfrac{\sqrt2\,(u^2-1)}{1+u^4}\), exactly \(A/du\).

Geometry on the canvas
  • \(U\) runs out along the \(45^\circ\) line. \(r_1\) and \(r_2\) join it to \(+1\) and \(-1\); at \(u=0\) both equal 1.
  • Law of cosines at the origin: the ray makes \(45^\circ\) with the axis towards \(+1\) and \(135^\circ\) towards \(-1\), so \(r_{1,2}^2=1+u^2\mp\sqrt2\,u\).
  • The small triangles on the unit circles about \(\pm1\) hold \(dr_1/r_1\) and \(dr_2/r_2\); stacking their differences multiplies lengths, so the total is the logarithm \(\ln(r_1/r_2)\), drawn as the gold bar.
\[\int\!\sqrt{\tan x}\,dx=\frac1{\sqrt2}\Big[\arctan\frac{\sqrt2\,u}{1-u^2}+\frac12\ln\frac{u^2-\sqrt2\,u+1}{u^2+\sqrt2\,u+1}\Big]+C\]
\[\frac{d}{du}\,\frac1{\sqrt2}\Big[\varphi+\ln\frac{r_1}{r_2}\Big]=\frac{\sqrt2\,(u^2+1)+\sqrt2\,(u^2-1)}{\sqrt2\,(1+u^4)}=\frac{2u^2}{1+u^4}\]
\[u=\sqrt{\tan x},\qquad u:0\to\infty\;\Rightarrow\;\ln\frac{r_1}{r_2}\to\ln1=0,\quad\varphi\to\pi\]
\[\int_0^{\pi/2}\!\sqrt{\tan x}\,dx=\frac1{\sqrt2}\,(0+\pi)\]
\[\boxed{\int_0^{\pi/2}\!\sqrt{\tan x}\,dx=\frac{\pi}{\sqrt2}}\]

Watch the bars: the log part \(\ln(r_1/r_2)\) dies away, the angle part \(\varphi\) climbs to \(\pi\), and the sum divided by \(\sqrt2\) lands on \(\pi/\sqrt2\approx2.2214\).

Geometry on the canvas
  • As \(U\) flies off to infinity along the \(45^\circ\) line, the distances \(r_1,r_2\) become almost equal: the gold bar \(\ln(r_1/r_2)\to0\).
  • The angle at \(U\) in triangle \((i,U,-i)\) shrinks to \(0\), so \(\varphi\) climbs to \(\pi\).
  • The white bar \(\Sigma=(A+B)/\sqrt2\) is the area under the curve so far, ending at \(\pi/\sqrt2\) (dashed marker).
