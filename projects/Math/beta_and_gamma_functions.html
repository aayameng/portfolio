1Factorials between the integers

THE IDEA IN PLAIN WORDS

We know n! only for whole numbers, which is a row of dots on a graph. Goal: draw one smooth curve through the dots, so that x! makes sense for any x. Many curves fit, so we add one shape rule (the curve of ln x! never bends downward). Exactly one curve passes through the dots and obeys it, and Euler’s limit builds that curve.

We want a smooth curve through $(0,1),(1,1),(2,2),(3,6),(4,24),\dots$, which means giving $x!$ a value when $x$ is not a whole number. For whole numbers, $(n+x)!$ can be grouped in two ways:

$$(n+x)! = n!\,(n+1)(n+2)\cdots(n+x) = x!\,(x+1)(x+2)\cdots(x+n).$$

Solve for $x!$, then multiply and divide by $n!\,n^{x}$:

$$x!=\frac{(n+x)!}{(x+1)\cdots(x+n)}=\underbrace{\frac{(n+x)!}{n!\,n^{x}}}_{\to\,1}\cdot\frac{n!\,n^{x}}{(x+1)(x+2)\cdots(x+n)}.$$

The first factor tends to $1$: $(n+x)!/n!=(n+1)\cdots(n+x)$ has $x$ factors, each about $n$ for large $n$, so it is about $n^x$. (This is the classical $n!=\lim_{x\to\infty}x^n/\binom xn$ with the letters swapped.) That gives Euler’s limit:

$$x!=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n!\,n^{x}}{(x+1)(x+2)\cdots(x+n)}.$$

The right side makes sense for any $x$, so we take it as the definition. Watch the curve snap onto the integer points as $n$ grows.

Notice that the dots alone do not decide the curve. The coloured curves are $x!\,(1+\varepsilon\sin 2\pi x)$ and similar: since $\sin 2\pi x=0$ at every whole number, they pass through every dot exactly, yet they swing above and below the gold curve in between. Drag “wiggle ε” to make them rowdier, or to flatten them back onto the true curve.

What rules them out is one extra shape rule, the Bohr–Mollerup theorem: the only function with $f(0)=1$, $f(x+1)=(x+1)f(x)$ and $\ln f$ convex (always bending upward) is the Euler limit. Press LOG SCALE: the gold curve $\ln x!$ bends upward everywhere, while every coloured curve has stretches that bend downward. Those stretches break log-convexity, so those curves are rejected.

Geometric insight: continuous interpolation

Infinitely many curves pass through the integer points. The Bohr–Mollerup theorem says this one is the only curve that is also log-convex. Switch on LOG SCALE: $\ln x!$ bends upward everywhere, with no wiggles between the points.

2Shift by one: the Gamma function

THE IDEA IN PLAIN WORDS

Goal: rename the curve so Γ(z) = (z−1)!, then see why the long product is well behaved. Take logarithms: a product becomes a sum of lengths, and the telescoping product becomes bars laid end to end on a ruler. Only the two ends of the ruler matter, so the whole product collapses.

Define $\Gamma(z)=(z-1)!=z!/z$. Put the limit above in the numerator:

$$\Gamma(z)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n!\,n^{z}}{z(z+1)(z+2)\cdots(z+n)}.$$

To see that the algebra is consistent, build the partial product $P_n=\prod_{k=1}^{n}\frac{(1+1/k)^{z-1}}{1+(z-1)/k}$. The numerator telescopes:

$$\prod_{k=1}^{n}\Big(1+\frac1k\Big)^{z-1}=\Big(\frac21\cdot\frac32\cdots\frac{n+1}{n}\Big)^{z-1}=(n+1)^{z-1}.$$

The denominator is a ratio of rising products:

$$\prod_{k=1}^{n}\frac{k+z-1}{k}=\frac{z}{1}\cdot\frac{z+1}{2}\cdots\frac{z+n-1}{n}=\frac{z(z+1)\cdots(z+n-1)}{n!}.$$ $$P_n=\frac{n!\,(n+1)^{z-1}}{z(z+1)\cdots(z+n-1)}\;\longrightarrow\;\Gamma(z),$$

because $(n+1)^{z-1}/n^{z-1}\to1$. This is the formula above with $n$ shifted by one.

Why this is the right picture: a logarithm turns a product into a sum of lengths. The factor $\frac{k+1}{k}$ becomes a bar of length $\ln\frac{k+1}{k}$, and each bar starts exactly where the previous one ended, so only the two outer endpoints $\ln1$ and $\ln(n+1)$ survive. Raising to the power $z-1$ multiplies every length by $z-1$, a uniform stretch of the whole ruler, which turns $n+1$ into $(n+1)^{z-1}$.

The denominator factors $1+\frac{z-1}{k}$ are nearly the same bars, each slightly longer or shorter depending on $z$. Their small mismatches add up to a finite amount, and that finite leftover is exactly what makes the limit $\Gamma(z)$ exist.

Each bar has length $\ln(1+\tfrac1k)$. The bars tile the ruler from $\ln1$ to $\ln(n+1)$ with no gaps or overlaps, and that is what telescoping means.

3Partial fractions

THE IDEA IN PLAIN WORDS

Goal: replace one fraction with n+1 poles by n+1 fractions with one pole each. Picture a landscape over the plane: each pole is a spike, and the whole function is the sum of the spikes. Next question: where do the coefficients (−1)^k C(n,k) come from? Answer: stand the unit cube on a corner and sweep a plane through it. The plane meets C(n,k) vertices at level k, and each vertex carries the sign (−1)^k.

Why split a fraction at all? The left side has $n+1$ poles at once, which makes it hard to integrate. Partial fractions rewrite it as a sum of simple pieces with one pole each, and each piece can be handled alone.

To turn the discrete product into an integral, split the fraction:

$$\frac{n!}{z(z+1)\cdots(z+n)}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{A_k}{z+k}.$$

Cover-up method: multiply both sides by $(z+k)$, then set $z=-k$. Every other term vanishes, so

$$A_k=\frac{n!}{\prod_{j\ne k}(j-k)}=\frac{n!}{\underbrace{(-k)\cdots(-1)}_{(-1)^k\,k!}\cdot\underbrace{1\cdot2\cdots(n-k)}_{(n-k)!}}=(-1)^k\binom nk.$$ $$\frac{n!}{z(z+1)\cdots(z+n)}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^k\binom nk\frac1{z+k}.$$

The coefficients are a row of Pascal’s triangle with alternating signs. In the panel, the thin curves are the individual terms and the gold curve is their sum. The vertical probe shows both sides agree.

Now lift it into 3D. Read $z$ as a point $x+iy$ of the plane and plot the height $\ln(1+|f|)$. Each term $\frac{A_k}{z+k}$ is a horn standing over its pole $z=-k$, and the ball at the pole shows the strength $|A_k|$. Slide “assemble” to add the horns one at a time; the gold surface is their sum. Drag to rotate.

Geometric insight: poles are the pieces

Near $z=-k$ every other term is smooth, so the surface looks exactly like the single horn $A_k/(z+k)$. That is why the cover-up method works: covering up $(z+k)$ and evaluating at $-k$ reads off the horn’s strength. Gold balls carry $+$ signs and blue balls $-$, the alternating signs of Pascal’s row.

The strengths $\binom nk$ also have a shape, and the shape explains the alternating signs. Take the unit cube $[0,1]^n$ and the plane $x_1+\cdots+x_n=s$. Stand the cube on one corner so the plane is a horizontal water level, then raise $s$ from $0$ to $n$. A vertex with $k$ coordinates equal to $1$ sits at height $k$, so the level touches exactly $\binom nk$ vertices when $s=k$. Each coordinate is a yes/no choice and $s$ counts the yeses, so sorting vertices by height is sorting choices by $k$. For $n=3$ the counts are $1,3,3,1$, and the slice goes triangle, hexagon, triangle.

Now the link to fractions. Integrate $(z+x_1+\cdots+x_n)^{-(n+1)}$ over the cube one coordinate at a time. Each step is “value at $x_i=0$ minus value at $x_i=1$”, and the constants from the antiderivatives, $\frac1n\cdot\frac1{n-1}\cdots\frac11$, collect into $\frac1{n!}$. After $n$ steps the cube has been replaced by its vertices ($k$ = number of coordinates equal to $1$):

$$\int_{[0,1]^n}\frac{dx}{(z+x_1+\cdots+x_n)^{n+1}}=\frac1{n!}\sum_{\text{vertices}}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{z+k}=\frac1{n!}\sum_{k=0}^n(-1)^k\binom nk\frac1{z+k}.$$

The integrand depends only on the height $k$, so the $2^n$ vertices collapse into levels of size $\binom nk$.

Geometric insight: why the signs alternate

Each vertex pays a toll of $-1$ for every coordinate sitting at the top endpoint. Level $k$ has $k$ such coordinates, so its sign is $(-1)^k$, and it holds $\binom nk$ vertices. By the cover-up result the whole expression equals $\frac1{z(z+1)\cdots(z+n)}$: the partial fractions are the cube’s vertices, read off along the diagonal.

4The integral bridge

THE IDEA IN PLAIN WORDS

Goal: turn the whole sum of fractions into one integral. Step one: each fraction 1/(z+k) is an area under a power curve. Step two: add the curves first and measure the area once. Step three: the binomial theorem, seen as a cube cut by three planes, folds the sum of curves into the single curve u^(z−1)(1−u)^n.

For $z+k>0$, each fraction is an area:

$$\int_0^1u^{z+k-1}\,du=\Big[\frac{u^{z+k}}{z+k}\Big]_0^1=\frac1{z+k}.$$
Geometric insight: areas equal fractions

The number $\frac1{z+k}$ is the exact area under $y=u^{z+k-1}$ on the unit interval, the same as a rectangle of width $1$ and height $\frac1a$ (dashed). Even when $a<1$, where the curve blows up at $0$, the area stays finite. Replacing fractions by areas lets us merge the whole sum into one shape.

Geometrically, each term is a signed area. Stacking the curves first and measuring once gives the same number as measuring each curve and adding, as long as there are finitely many of them. The shared factor $u^{z-1}$ is a common height multiplier, so it can be pulled out of the stack.

Substitute into the sum. It is finite, so sum and integral may be swapped, and the common factor $u^{z-1}$ comes out:

$$\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^k\binom nk\int_0^1u^{z+k-1}du=\int_0^1u^{z-1}\underbrace{\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom nk(-u)^k}_{(1-u)^n\ \text{(binomial theorem)}}du.$$ $$\frac{n!}{z(z+1)\cdots(z+n)}=\int_0^1u^{z-1}(1-u)^n\,du.$$

The same cube gives the binomial theorem. Cut it with the planes $x=u$, $y=u$, $z=u$. The corner box where every coordinate is at least $u$ has side $1-u$, so its volume is $(1-u)^n$. Count it the other way, by inclusion–exclusion: start with the whole cube ($1$), remove the $n$ slabs $\{x_i<u\}$ (volume $u$ each), add back the $\binom n2$ pairwise overlaps (volume $u^2$ each), remove the triple overlaps, and so on. That total is $\sum_k\binom nk(-u)^k$.

Geometric insight: the binomial theorem is a volume

$\binom nk$ counts the ways to choose $k$ slabs, $u^k$ is the volume where those $k$ slabs overlap, and $(-1)^k$ is the inclusion–exclusion sign. Both counts measure the same gold box.

The area under the curve matches the product, to every digit shown. This is Euler’s Beta integral $B(z,n+1)$.

The two cube pictures are one. Put $\frac1{(z+s)^{n+1}}=\frac1{n!}\int_0^\infty w^ne^{-(z+s)w}\,dw$ into the cube integral from Step 3. The cube integral of $e^{-w(x_1+\cdots+x_n)}$ factors into $n$ equal one-dimensional integrals, each $\int_0^1e^{-wx}dx=\frac{1-e^{-w}}{w}$, so the powers of $w$ cancel:

$$\frac1{z(z+1)\cdots(z+n)}=\frac1{n!}\int_0^\infty e^{-zw}(1-e^{-w})^n\,dw=\frac1{n!}\int_0^1u^{z-1}(1-u)^n\,du,\qquad u=e^{-w}.$$

Multiply by $n!$ and the Beta integral appears a second way: the diagonal plane supplies the fractions, and the factor $(1-e^{-w})^n$ is the binomial theorem on the same cube.

5Stretching the domain

THE IDEA IN PLAIN WORDS

Goal: stop the hump from shrinking as n grows. The factor (1−u)^n pushes all the area against u = 0. Stretch the horizontal axis by n and shrink the heights by n: the area is unchanged, but the hump no longer collapses and settles into a fixed shape on the window [0, n].

Insert the integral into $\Gamma$: $\Gamma(z)=\lim_{n\to\infty}n^z\int_0^1u^{z-1}(1-u)^n\,du$. Substitute $u=t/n$, so $du=dt/n$, with $u:0\to1$ becoming $t:0\to n$:

$$n^{z}\int_0^n\Big(\frac tn\Big)^{z-1}\Big(1-\frac tn\Big)^{n}\frac{dt}{n}=n^{\,z-(z-1)-1}\int_0^n t^{z-1}\Big(1-\frac tn\Big)^{n}dt=\int_0^n t^{z-1}\Big(1-\frac tn\Big)^{n}dt.$$

The powers of $n$ cancel exactly: $z-(z-1)-1=0$. Slide $s$ from $0$ to $1$ to dilate the window by the factor $n^s$.

Why must $n^z$ be there? As $n$ grows, $(1-u)^n$ squeezes the hump against $u=0$, and the total area shrinks like $n^{-z}$. Multiplying by $n^z$ restores it. The dilation does the same job geometrically: stretching the width by $n$ undoes the squeeze, so the hump stops running away and settles into a fixed shape.

Geometric insight: domain dilation

The map $(u,y)\mapsto(nu,\,y/n)$ stretches the picture horizontally by $n$ and squashes it vertically by $n$, so the area never changes. As $n\to\infty$ the window $[0,n]$ grows to cover the whole half-line $[0,\infty)$.

6The exponential limit

THE IDEA IN PLAIN WORDS

Goal: identify the limiting shape. For a fixed t, (1−t/n)^n is compound interest with a negative rate: it rises toward e^(−t) as n grows and never crosses it. So the area under the stretched hump creeps up to the area under t^(z−1)e^(−t).

$$\Gamma(z)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_0^n t^{z-1}\Big(1-\frac tn\Big)^{n}dt.$$

For fixed $t$, expand the logarithm:

$$\Big(1-\frac tn\Big)^n=\exp\!\Big(n\ln\!\big(1-\tfrac tn\big)\Big)=\exp\!\Big(-t-\frac{t^2}{2n}-\cdots\Big)\;\longrightarrow\;e^{-t}.$$

Moving the limit inside the integral is legitimate. Since $\ln(1-x)\le-x$, we have $0\le(1-t/n)^n\le e^{-t}$ on $[0,n]$, and $t^{z-1}e^{-t}$ is integrable for $\operatorname{Re}z>0$. Dominated convergence applies. Press play and watch $n$ run from 1 to 200.

Why the area only creeps up to its limit: for every $t$, $(1-t/n)^n$ rises as $n$ increases but never crosses above $e^{-t}$. The gold curve always sits inside the dashed one and creeps toward it, so the area under gold never overshoots $\Gamma(z)$, and widening the window to $[0,\infty)$ adds the remaining tail.

Geometric insight: the exponential limit

For small $n$, $(1-t/n)^n$ is a polynomial that hits zero at $t=n$. Each increase in $n$ smooths the drop and pushes the end farther out, and the family converges to the decay curve $e^{-t}$. It is compound interest, compounded continuously, applied to area.

7The result

THE IDEA IN PLAIN WORDS

Goal: read the final formula as a picture and test it. The curve t^(z−1) is a ramp or spike, e^(−t) is a decaying slope, and their product is a hump. Γ(z) is the area under that hump. Check: at z = 1 the area is exactly 1, and each increase of z by 1 multiplies the area by z, which is the factorial rule.

Reading the formula as a picture: $e^{-t}$ is a decaying slope and $t^{z-1}$ is a ramp (for $z>1$) or a spike at $0$ (for $z<1$). Their product is a hump peaking at $t=z-1$, and $\Gamma(z)$ is the whole area under it. At $z=1$ the ramp is flat and the area is exactly $1$. Larger $z$ pushes the hump to the right and makes it taller and wider, which is how the area keeps growing like $(z-1)!$.

$$\Gamma(z)=\int_0^\infty t^{z-1}e^{-t}\,dt,\qquad \operatorname{Re}z>0.$$

Sanity checks: $\Gamma(1)=\int_0^\infty e^{-t}dt=1$. Integration by parts gives $\Gamma(z+1)=z\,\Gamma(z)$, so $\Gamma(n+1)=n!$, and the curve in Step 1 is exactly this integral.

1Anatomy of the formula

THE IDEA IN PLAIN WORDS

Goal: read the identity piece by piece before proving it. Two curves, one falling and one rising, always multiply to the same trigonometric curve. Later steps turn that product into a length, an area and a mirror image.

$$\Gamma(z)\,\Gamma(1-z)=\frac{\pi}{\sin \pi z},\qquad 0< z< 1.$$

There are four pieces. $\Gamma(z)=\int_0^\infty t^{z-1}e^{-t}dt$ blows up like $1/z$ as $z\to0^+$. $\Gamma(1-z)$ is its mirror image across $z=\tfrac12$, so it blows up as $z\to1^-$. The constant $\pi$ is the length of the unit semicircle. Finally $\sin\pi z$ is zero at every integer, so $\pi/\sin\pi z$ has a pole at every integer, exactly where one of the two Gamma factors has one.

Geometric insight: a U-shaped balance

Where one factor is huge the other is close to $1$. The product is a symmetric U-shaped curve with its minimum $\pi$ at $z=\tfrac12$, where both factors equal $\sqrt\pi$.

2Algebra: factorials produce a sine

THE IDEA IN PLAIN WORDS

Goal: prove the identity. Write both factors as Euler limits. The opposite powers of $n$ combine to a harmless $n$, and the leftover factors pair up as $(k+z)(k-z)=k^2-z^2$. That is the shape of Euler’s product for the sine, whose zeros are the integers.

Euler’s limit, applied to $z$ and to $1-z$, gives

$$\Gamma(z)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n!\,n^{z}}{z(z+1)\cdots(z+n)},\qquad \Gamma(1-z)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n!\,n^{1-z}}{(1-z)(2-z)\cdots(n+1-z)}.$$

Multiply them. The powers give $n^z\cdot n^{1-z}=n$. In the denominator, $(k+z)$ from the first factor meets $(k-z)$ from the second for $k=1,\dots,n$, leaving $z$ and $(n+1-z)$ over:

$$\Gamma(z)\Gamma(1-z)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n}{n+1-z}\cdot\frac{(n!)^2}{z\prod_{k=1}^{n}(k^2-z^2)}=\frac{1}{z\prod_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{z^2}{k^2}\right)}.$$

Here $n/(n+1-z)\to1$, and $(n!)^2=\prod k^2$ divides each $k^2-z^2$ into $1-z^2/k^2$. Euler’s sine product is $\sin\pi z=\pi z\prod_{k\ge1}\left(1-\frac{z^2}{k^2}\right)$, so the denominator is $\sin(\pi z)/\pi$:

$$\Gamma(z)\Gamma(1-z)=\frac{\pi}{\sin\pi z}.$$
Geometric insight: one factor, one pair of poles

Each factor $1-z^2/k^2$ vanishes at $z=\pm k$, so it adds a pair of poles to the reciprocal. Raise $n$ and the poles march outward along the integers, locking the gold curve onto $\pi/\sin\pi z$.

3Model I: arc over chord

THE IDEA IN PLAIN WORDS

Goal: turn the identity into a ratio of two lengths. Multiply by $z$ so the left side becomes $z!\,(-z)!$. The right side $\frac{\pi z}{\sin\pi z}$ is then the curved length of a circular arc divided by the straight length of its chord.

Since $z\,\Gamma(z)=\Gamma(1+z)=z!$ and $\Gamma(1-z)=(-z)!$, multiplying the identity by $z$ gives

$$z!\,(-z)!=\frac{\pi z}{\sin\pi z}=\frac{2\pi z}{2\sin\pi z}.$$

On a unit circle, a central angle $\theta=2\pi z$ cuts an arc of length $s=R\theta=2\pi z$. Splitting the sector down the middle makes two right triangles with hypotenuse $1$ and angle $\pi z$, so each half-chord is $\sin\pi z$ and the full chord is $c=2\sin\pi z$. Hence $z!\,(-z)!=s/c$.

Geometric insight: a curve is never shorter than its straight line

The arc is always longer than the chord, so $z!\,(-z)!\ge1$. At $z=\tfrac12$ the arc is a semicircle ($\pi$) over a diameter ($2$), giving $(\tfrac12)!\,(-\tfrac12)!=\tfrac\pi2$, which proves $\Gamma(\tfrac12)=\sqrt\pi$. As $z\to0^+$ a tiny arc hugs its chord and the ratio tends to $1=0!\,0!$.

4Model II: area inside a superellipse

THE IDEA IN PLAIN WORDS

Goal: turn the identity into an area. Multiply by $z(1-z)$ and the left side becomes $\Gamma(1+z)\Gamma(2-z)$. The Beta integral from the Gamma derivation says that this product is the area under a curve $x^p+y^q=1$ in the unit square, with exponents $p=1/z$ and $q=1/(1-z)$.

Because $z\Gamma(z)=\Gamma(1+z)$ and $(1-z)\Gamma(1-z)=\Gamma(2-z)$,

$$\Gamma(1+z)\,\Gamma(2-z)=\frac{\pi z(1-z)}{\sin\pi z}.$$

The exponents satisfy $\frac1p+\frac1q=z+(1-z)=1$. Solve $y=(1-x^p)^{1/q}$ and substitute $u=x^p$, so $dx=\frac1p u^{1/p-1}du$:

$$\text{Area}=\int_0^1(1-x^p)^{1/q}dx=\frac1p\int_0^1u^{\frac1p-1}(1-u)^{\frac1q}du=\frac{\Gamma(1+\frac1p)\,\Gamma(1+\frac1q)}{\Gamma(1+\frac1p+\frac1q)}=\frac{\Gamma(1+z)\Gamma(2-z)}{\Gamma(2)}.$$

Since $\Gamma(2)=1$, the area equals $\pi z(1-z)/\sin\pi z$.

Geometric insight: the quarter circle is the smallest member

At $z=\tfrac12$ we get $p=q=2$, a quarter circle of area $\pi/4$. Moving $z$ toward $0$ or $1$ pushes one corner out until the curve fills the whole unit square (area $1$).

5Model III: the 180° reflection

THE IDEA IN PLAIN WORDS

Goal: see why this is called a reflection formula. The map $z\mapsto1-z$ spins the complex plane by $180^\circ$ about the point $\tfrac12$. It swaps the poles of $\Gamma(z)$ with the poles of $\Gamma(1-z)$, so together they cover every integer, just like $\sin\pi z$.

$$1-z=\tfrac12-\left(z-\tfrac12\right).$$

Whatever distance $z$ sits from $\tfrac12$ in one direction, $1-z$ sits the same distance in the opposite direction. Reflecting the poles of $\Gamma(z)$ lands on the poles of $\Gamma(1-z)$:

$$\{0,-1,-2,\dots\}\ \longleftrightarrow\ \{1,2,3,\dots\}.$$

On the real line, this explains the U in Step 1: as $z$ moves right, $\Gamma(z)$ falls and its mirror partner $\Gamma(1-z)$ rises by exactly the mirrored amount.

Geometric insight: a half-turn about ½

Drag the point $z$ anywhere and press PLAY on the rotate slider. The point swings around the pivot $\tfrac12$ and lands on $1-z$. Gold poles (of $\Gamma(z)$) sit on the left of the pivot and blue poles (of $\Gamma(1-z)$) on the right, and each gold pole has a blue partner.
